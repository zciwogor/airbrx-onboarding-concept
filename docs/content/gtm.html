Executive summary

Airbrx gets to its first 10 customers by the Dec 17 pitch by finding Snowflake, Databricks and Postgres teams already feeling cost pain, proving savings on one workload, and putting them on a paid plan. Revenue grows from there through flat plans, metered enterprise contracts and forward-deployed engineering (FDE) work.

The plan rests on four bets:

  1. Sell to pain, prove it fast. Teams with real cost pain pay. The free tier's cap sits low enough that they reach it. Every company pilots free with no charge up front: 30 days by default, extended up to 90 days to help close, especially smaller companies. Once they see the savings, a usage cap moves them to a paid plan. A small free tier for startups and very small companies gets us in the door early, spreads the name and collects real-world data. The free year is a last resort only. Customers move up a plan when they need more tenants, warehouses, users or features.

  2. Sell to the check writer, win the engineer. The CFO or VP Data hears a bill story in their own unit (credits, DBUs, compute). The engineer hears "one hostname change, fails to pass-through."

  3. Try it, don't buy it. "Try it" opens doors without pushy selling; cost pain is what turns that into a paid deal. Abram warms up, Amit closes, FDE work follows.

  4. Learn as we sell. Every conversation updates a customer profile. Profiles drive messaging, pricing and the roadmap.

The funnel

A prospect becomes a customer when they start a 30 to 90 day pilot with live traffic flowing, and a paying customer when the pilot ends and the usage cap moves them to a plan. Startups on the free tier sit at stage 4 too and are tracked separately until they grow into a plan.

The free scan is an optional side door at stage 2: it gives cautious buyers a savings estimate before they connect. Stage counts live in the CRM; the Metrics section sets November targets.

Alternatives and how we win

Our toughest competitor is "we already cache" or "we'll tune the warehouse," not another startup. Airbrx wins where repeat traffic comes from dashboards and agents, where data changes often, and where setup has to be one hostname change.

      • Outstanding Automated routing: no confirmed intent to build it.

  • Add "What else did you look at?" to every profile so win and loss reasons build up.

  • Watch Snowflake and Databricks cache changes, and Cube's agent features.

Pricing and packaging

The full model lives in the Airbrx Pricing Plan: plans, features by plan, billing rules, savings measurement and open decisions. That doc is the single source of truth; this section covers only what sales needs.

Status: revised Nov 3. On Nov 3, Michael and Ben questioned tying price to savings: it can imply the team has been wasting money and invites "we'll fix it ourselves." The Pricing Plan now keeps fixed prices for most teams, steps plans up by tenants, warehouses, users and features, keeps savings on the statement as proof, and makes the free tier free forever with a usage cap. Sales talks about the pilot, fixed plans and the savings statement. Founders still need to approve it.

What sales can say now

  • Every company pilots free: 30 days, extendable to 90 to help close. No card.

  • At the end of the pilot, the savings statement shows what Airbrx saved and the plan that fits.

  • Most paid plans are fixed monthly prices from $299. Larger accounts: "let's talk."

  • Startups and very small teams can stay on the free tier forever, under a usage cap.

  • Don't say: savings percentages, "you pay only when you save", warehouse routing (an open question, not an offer), or the free year (last resort, founder approval).

Objection answers

"Do you hold our credentials or data?"Credentials pass through, never stored. Cache is encrypted and never inspected. Large accounts can self-host.

Friction already seen (the evidence behind the drafts):

  • Per-GB pricing confuses buyers who think in credits or DBUs.

  • Engineers skip the second call; setup has to happen on the first.

  • The free scan asks people to run SQL from a website, which is hard to trust cold.

  • The rules engine is hard to understand; having the customer's own AI write rules through the MCP is the likely fix.

  • Switching tenants in the admin app means going back to the tenant tab.
